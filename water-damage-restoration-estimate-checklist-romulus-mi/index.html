What a Water Damage Restoration Estimate Should Include in Romulus, MI.

An estimate is more than a number. The line items show whether the plan matches the damage.

Call (844) 950-6900

By the Romulus Water Damage Pros team. Updated November 2026.

What an estimate should tell you.

A useful estimate says what is wet, what will be removed, what will be dried, how long equipment will run and what repair, if any, is included. It also explains what could change the price once work begins.

If an estimate is a single number with no detail, ask for the breakdown before you approve.

Fixed price, itemized or time and materials.

Some estimates are a fixed price for a defined scope, while others itemize each step and a few bill by time and materials. Each has trade-offs.

A fixed price is easy to compare but may hide assumptions, itemized is transparent but can grow if the scope grows and time and materials gives flexibility with less certainty. Ask which applies and what triggers a change.

Questions to ask before you sign.

  1. What could make this price go up, and how will I be told?
  2. Are equipment days counted daily and how many do you expect?
  3. Who removes and disposes of materials?
  4. What documentation will I get for my insurer?
  5. Is repair included or priced separately?

Hidden costs and how to spot them.

Disposal fees, equipment days beyond the first few and after hours charges are common places where totals grow. Ask whether disposal is included, how equipment days are counted and whether nights or weekends change the rate.

Contents handling, such as moving furniture or packing belongings, is often priced separately. If you need it, ask for it in writing.

None of these items is unreasonable on its own, but they should appear on the estimate before work starts.

What your insurer may ask about the estimate.

Adjusters often compare an estimate to their own scope, so a clear itemized document helps. Expect questions about why certain materials were removed and how drying was measured.

Keep moisture logs, photos and the written estimate together. That package answers most questions without a back and forth.

Questions about the timeline.

Ask how many visits are planned, when drying readings will be shared and when the structure will be ready for repair. A realistic schedule includes the days equipment runs and the days between drying and rebuild.

If a company gives you a very short timeline for a large loss, ask what assumptions it is making and what happens if readings do not reach the goal.

A last check before you approve.

Read the estimate once more with the damage in front of you. Does each wet room appear, does the drying plan match the water category and do the exclusions make sense? If anything surprises you, call before you sign.

Keep a signed copy, photograph each page and store it with your claim records.

Checking an estimate against the published standard.

A good estimate uses the terms of the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, the published reference for professional water damage restoration. Look for the water category, the loss class and the drying goals.

If those are missing, ask why. The standard is an industry document and not a certificate.

Romulus note: what local storms mean for estimates.

Romulus note: official weather and insurance facts.

The National Weather Service recorded 3.31 inches of rain in Romulus in about an hour and a half on July 28, 2025, and many homes would have needed similar scopes at once. When demand spikes, ask whether the estimate assumes normal conditions and whether any items are subject to availability.

Michigan DIFS advises policyholders to report claims and take photos or video, so share the estimate with your insurer and keep photos of the damage.

Comparing two estimates fairly.

Put them side by side and check that rooms, materials and drying plans match. If one is much lower, look for what is missing, and if one is much higher, look for extras.

When the estimate changes.

A scope can change when crews open a wall and find more water than expected. A good company calls you, shows photos and explains what changed before the work continues.

Ask for the change in writing, and keep it with the original estimate so your insurer sees the whole picture.

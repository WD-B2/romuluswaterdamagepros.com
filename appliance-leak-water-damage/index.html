An appliance is flooding: what to do right now.

If a washer, dishwasher or refrigerator line is leaking, shut off the valve behind the appliance, unplug it only if the cord and floor are dry, and call our 24/7 line. Keep out of standing water that touches outlets.

These leaks tend to be clean water at first, which is the easiest category to dry. They grow expensive when they run for hours, such as overnight or while you are at work.

Appliances leak on weekends and at night too, so we treat those calls as normal. Same-day response is what we work toward when crews are available, and the person on the phone will tell you what to expect.

Water from appliances moves out of sight.

Flooring that looks fine can hide a wet subfloor, especially under vinyl and hardwood. Water can travel under cabinets and along the wall base to the next room.

We use moisture meters and thermal imaging to follow it. That is why a quick wipe up is not enough, because a damp subfloor can cup flooring and loosen adhesives weeks later.

What we do after an appliance leak.

  1. Pull the appliance.

    If needed we move it out so we can reach the wet floor and wall base.

  2. Extract and open up.

    We remove standing water and lift pad or toe kicks so air can reach wet areas.

  3. Dry.

    Air movers and a dehumidifier run while we record moisture readings.

  4. Repair.

    Flooring, baseboard or cabinet sections are replaced if drying does not bring them back.

Prevention that actually helps.

Braided steel hoses hold up better than old rubber ones, and replacing hoses on a schedule is cheap compared with a flood. Leak sensors under the washer or fridge can alert your phone, and shutting valves off when you travel is a simple habit.

Many homeowners never check these valves until a leak, so find yours now and make sure they turn.

What to record for your insurer.

Take photos of the appliance, the hose or valve that failed and the wet floor. Note the brand, model and age if you can find them, and keep the failed part in a bag.

Write down when you noticed the leak and when you shut the water off. Those details help your insurer understand whether the event was sudden.

What the S500 standard says about appliance leaks.

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the published reference for water damage restoration, and it sorts water by source and by how far it spread. A supply line failure counts differently from a dishwasher drain that carried food waste.

We cite the standard as the industry guide our process follows. It is not a claim that we hold any certification, and it never replaces what your meter readings show on the day.

What affects the cost.

Cost factors.

The main factors are the amount of water, the flooring type, whether it reached a basement or a lower floor and how long it sat. A quick catch on tile costs far less than an overnight leak over wood.

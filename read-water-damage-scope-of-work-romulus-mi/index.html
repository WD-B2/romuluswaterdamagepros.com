How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Romulus, MI.

A scope of work is the plan in writing. Reading it carefully before you sign is the best protection a homeowner has.

Call (844) 950-6900

By the Romulus Water Damage Pros team. Updated November 2026.

What a scope of work is.

A scope of work lists what a contractor will do, where, with what equipment and under what conditions. It is the document your insurer, your contractor and you can all point to when questions come up.

It is different from a price quote, although the two are often combined. The scope says what will happen, and the price says what it will cost.

The sections you should see.

Not every company uses these exact headings, but each idea should appear somewhere.

Terms you will see.

Extraction is removing water, and demolition or tear out means removing damaged materials. Drying covers air movers, dehumidifiers and monitoring, and containment means sealing off an area, usually with plastic.

Antimicrobial application is a treatment applied to surfaces, and monitoring is the daily readings that show progress. Reconstruction or repair means putting back what was removed.

If a term is unfamiliar, ask for a plain description of what will physically happen.

Red flags in a scope.

  • A single lump sum with no tasks listed.
  • Words like as needed without any limit or approval step.
  • No mention of drying readings or documentation.
  • Pressure to sign before you have time to read.
  • A statement about what your insurer will pay.

A short example of how a line reads.

A clear line item reads something like this: remove and dispose of wet carpet pad in the living room, about one hundred and twenty square feet, due to water from a supply line break. It names the task, the place, the size and the reason.

A vague line reads something like this: demo as needed. It tells you nothing about what will leave your house or why, so ask for it to be rewritten with specifics.

Keeping your scope useful over time.

Store the scope, the change orders and the final invoice together. If a leak returns or a buyer asks about past repairs years later, the file tells the story.

Take a photo of each page, and email a copy to yourself so you are not relying on a paper copy that could get wet.

Where the published standard fits in a scope.

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the published reference many scopes are built on. When a scope lists water categories or loss classes, those terms come from that standard.

Knowing this helps you ask what category and class the company assigned and why. Citing the standard is not a claim that anyone holds a certificate.

Matching the scope to the damage.

Walk through the house with the scope in hand. Check that each wet room is listed, that the demolition matches what you saw and that the drying plan covers the lower levels as well.

Change orders and added work.

Crews sometimes find more water than expected once walls are open. A good company stops, shows you photos and writes up the change before continuing.

Ask for every change in writing. Keep the original scope and each update together, since they tell the whole story of the job.

Before you sign.

  1. Read every page and ask about words you do not know.
  2. Check the address, the date and the loss description.
  3. Confirm the drying plan includes daily readings.
  4. Ask what is excluded and who handles it.
  5. Keep a signed copy and a photo of the pages.

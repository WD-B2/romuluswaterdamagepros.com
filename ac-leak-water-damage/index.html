How an air conditioner ends up leaking inside the house.

Cooling coils pull moisture out of the air, and that water is supposed to leave through a condensate drain line. When the line clogs, the pan overflows or the unit freezes and thaws, the water goes into the ceiling, wall or floor instead.

The damage is usually gradual. A small drip over many hot weeks can soak insulation and stain drywall before anyone sees a drop.

What restoration covers and what the HVAC technician does.

We remove wet insulation and drywall, dry the cavity and rebuild the ceiling or wall. An HVAC technician clears the line, replaces the pan or repairs the unit, which stops the water at the source.

The order matters. If the unit keeps dripping, the new drywall will stain again, so ask the technician to finish before we close the wall.

How we handle an AC leak job.

  1. Find the path.

    We trace the water from the stain back to the air handler or line with a meter and thermal camera.

  2. Remove the wet material.

    Soaked insulation and drywall are cut out in measured sections.

  3. Dry.

    Air movers and dehumidifiers dry framing and the surrounding room.

  4. Rebuild.

    New drywall, texture and paint finish the ceiling after the technician confirms the leak is fixed.

Why this is not usually an emergency, and when it is.

Most AC leaks are slow, so there is time to plan, though you should still call soon because wet insulation holds moisture against framing. If the pan is overflowing or water is dripping through a light fixture, it becomes urgent and the 24/7 line is open.

Turn the system off, which stops more condensate, and keep power off for any wet fixture unless you can reach the panel safely.

The usual causes behind a condensate leak.

  • A clogged drain line, often from algae or debris.
  • A cracked or rusted drain pan under the coil.
  • A dirty air filter that lets the coil freeze and later melt.
  • A drain line that lost its slope or disconnected.
  • A secondary pan or float switch that failed to shut the unit off.

Attics, closets and basements.

Where the air handler sits changes the damage. In an attic the water soaks insulation and ceiling drywall first, in a closet it hits the floor and wall, and in a basement it usually reaches the floor drain or flooring.

We start at the unit and work outward, because the wet area can be larger than the stain. Readings show where to stop cutting.

How to prevent a repeat.

Have the line cleared and the pan checked each cooling season, change filters on schedule and ask the technician about a float switch that cuts power when water appears.

Prevention costs far less than a ceiling, and it is a good habit even when nothing has leaked yet.

How the published standard applies to AC leaks.

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for professional water damage restoration is the industry reference for classifying water and planning drying. An AC condensate leak is usually clean water at first, but the standard treats standing or slow leaks as water that can turn worse over time.

This is a statement about the industry document we follow as a guide, not a claim about any certificate. When a ceiling or wall is wet from a drain line, we use the standard to decide how far to measure and when drying goals are met.

What affects the cost.

Cost factors.

Cost follows the size of the wet area, whether insulation and joists are affected and the finish on the ceiling. Attic units can mean more insulation removal than a closet unit.

Insurance and related services.

Policies vary on gradual leaks versus sudden events, so ask your insurer and describe the loss as water damage. Keep photos of the unit, the line and the stain. See ceiling water damage repair for the finish work and structural drying for the moisture step.

When water hits a shop, office or warehouse, every hour of downtime costs money. We extract, dry and document so you can reopen.

Water in your building after hours.

A commercial water loss is an emergency, and our 24/7 line is open for it. Shut off water at the main if you can, secure the area, keep staff and customers out of wet spaces and call so we can start while the loss is small.

Tell the person on the phone the building type, the floor material and what is stored near the water. That helps plan equipment and keep inventory safe.

Same-day response is what we work toward when crews are available, including nights and weekends, and the person on the phone will tell you what to expect.

Commercial losses differ from homes.

Buildings are larger, floors are often concrete or tile, and downtime has a direct cost. Tenants and owners share responsibility, so the lease often decides who calls and who pays.

We work with you on that, document the loss for the insurer or landlord and plan around hours you need to reopen.

How a commercial job runs.

  1. Secure the site.

    Utilities are checked and the affected area is marked off.

  2. Extract and protect.

    Extractors remove water while inventory and equipment are lifted or covered.

  3. Dry and monitor.

    Air movers and dehumidifiers run with daily readings and written logs.

  4. Rebuild in phases.

    Repairs can be staged so the rest of the business keeps running.

Around Romulus.

Romulus has an active commercial mix, from the Wayne Road corridor to airport-related businesses along Merriman Road and the Vining Road Development District. Large logistics buildings and shared walls mean losses can spread between tenants, so we ask about the building layout early.

What we ask on the first call.

The first call sets the plan, so a few details help. Tell us the type of building and approximate square footage, the floor material, what is stored near the water and whether tenants share walls or ceilings.

We also ask whether power is on in the wet area, whether the sprinkler or main water has been shut off and when you need the space usable again. Those answers decide equipment, crew size and sequence.

Small businesses near downtown are a common example, and our page for Five Points and downtown Romulus explains how we handle shared walls and rented space.

Documentation for owners, tenants and insurers.

Commercial claims involve more parties, so paperwork matters. We keep photos, moisture logs and a written scope that show what was wet, what was removed and what was dried, and we share them on request.

If you are a tenant, the lease often controls who can authorize work. Share the relevant section with your landlord or property manager early so approvals do not slow the dry-out.

Reducing downtime after a loss.

Where possible we stage the work, drying the critical area first and leaving noncritical rooms for later. Containment barriers keep dust and equipment noise away from areas still operating.

Once readings reach drying goals, repair can be scheduled for evenings or slower days. That plan is something we work out with you, because every business has its own schedule.

Standards behind commercial drying plans.

ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published standard for professional water damage restoration, and commercial jobs lean on it because it gives owners, managers and insurers a common language. Water categories and loss classes let everyone talk about the same job in the same terms.

We cite the standard as an industry document that guides the plan. It is not a claim that we hold a certificate, and it does not replace the written scope for your building.

What to do and what not to do while you wait.

If it is safe, stop the water at the shutoff and keep people out of wet areas. Do not touch outlets, panels or equipment in standing water, and do not move stock back into a wet room.

Photograph the damage and note which areas are in use. Then call the 24/7 line so the plan can start.

What affects the cost.

Cost factors.

Cost follows the square footage affected, flooring, how many days equipment runs and whether inventory or finishes are involved. Access hours and phased work can add steps.
